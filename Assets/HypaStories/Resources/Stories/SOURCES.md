# Elder story sources

The elder offers Hypa retellings of documented Cameroon and Edo/Bini tales. These are adaptations, not transcriptions or claims to preserve an original-language performance. The English scripts, narration questions, practice words, and translations were prepared for this app.

## The Death of the Rice Farmer

- Tradition: Nso, North West Region, Cameroon. The source records the Lamnso title as **lim san kpu ee**.
- Source: *Folktales as indigenous pedagogic tools for educating school children: A mixed methods study among the Nso of Cameroon*, Frontiers in Psychology (2023), section 3.1.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1049691
- The published account describes a farmer using an animal's entrails to pretend to be dead, catch the birds eating his rice, and protect his field. This concise retelling adds narrative transitions and a closing lesson, without adding new named characters or claiming a verbatim traditional text.

## The Girl Who Chose Hare

- Tradition: **Medumba**, Cameroon. The earlier description of this tale as Babanki was incorrect.
- Source: Pius W. Akumbu, Roland Kießling, Constantine Kouankem, Justine G. Nzweundji, and Cornelius W. Wuchu, *Integrating traditional stories in formal education in the Cameroonian Grassfields*, Journal of the Cameroon Academy of Sciences 21(3), 205-228 (2025), p. 217, the account of **Hare and Girl with a Wound**.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.4314/jcas.v21i3.4
- The girl is injured gathering firewood, abandoned by jealous companions, and rescued after a hunter brings her song to the village. Hare heals her wound; she chooses him over wealthy or high-ranking suitors. The app title is an English adaptation. The tale's traditional healing is part of the story; the retelling gives no treatment instructions.

Both publications identify their text as Creative Commons Attribution 4.0: https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/. Credit the publications and communities when redistributing these adaptations. The seven translation files per story adapt the English retelling into the existing app languages; they are not Lamnso or Medumba recordings. Native-speaker editorial review has not been performed.

## Joromi and the Spirit Wrestlers

- Tradition: Edo/Bini, Benin City, Nigeria. This is a wrestling legend; the app does not present modern boxing as its original sport.
- Source: John Collins, *Victor Uwaifo: Highlife Pioneer, Legend* (2011), chapter four, describing the Joromi account supplied by Victor Uwaifo in *Expo '98: Victor Uwaifo — The Legend*, p. 13.
- Link: https://www.academia.edu/32340860/JCollins_VICTOR_UWAIFO_HIGHLIFE_PIONEER_LEGEND_Unpublished_photo_captions_are_included_but_no_photos_provided
- Corroborating account: Afropop Worldwide, *Sir Victor Uwaifo, Superstar: In His Own Words* (2017): https://old.afropop.org/articles/uwaifo
- Endings vary. Hypa's independently written, roughly four-minute retelling follows the escape variant documented by Collins: the champion climbs a forbidden palm, his sister follows as a fly, he fights multi-headed opponents, and returns from the spirit world. Narrative transitions and the closing reflection on pride are Hypa's additions. The app uses no Uwaifo lyrics, poem, recording, or melody.
- The four practice spirits (Mist, Ember, Wind, and the seven-headed final opponent), their appearance, boxing patterns, and scoring are game adaptations. They are not a claim about traditional Edo combat rules or named spirits. The practice does not replace the narrated ending.
- All spirit meshes are original procedural app art. The seven translations, questions, practice words, and game labels were generated through Hypa's `translate_message` MCP tool. They are not Edo-language source performances; native-speaker editorial review is still pending.
